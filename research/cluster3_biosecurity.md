## 1. Synthetic Biology Biosecurity

### Key Findings
- The NSABB Working Group on Synthetic Genomics (Relman, 2007) concluded that synthetic biology should be subject to institutional review and oversight, as some aspects pose biosecurity risks not covered by existing frameworks for recombinant DNA.
- Jin et al. (2021) argue that biosecurity policies and practices must be updated to accommodate novel challenges from synthetic biology, including gene editing and genetic engineering proliferation.
- The "bottom-up" approach (creating artificial life from scratch) remains in infancy and does not currently pose significant biosecurity concerns beyond existing oversight, but is advancing rapidly and requires regular re-assessment.

## 2. Gene Drive Governance

### Key Findings
- Gene drives have two distinguishing features: they intentionally spread a genetic trait through a population, and they challenge existing GMO governance systems designed for contained organisms.
- The US Coordinated Framework shares regulatory authority across FDA, USDA, and EPA, but existing mechanisms are insufficient for gene drives due to their intentional population spread.
- Warmbrod et al. (2022) propose a tiered registry system (similar to ClinicalTrials.gov) for gene drive governance, providing transparency for researchers, government officials, and the public.
- iGEM's case study (Millett et al., 2021) distills 6 key insights for gene drive policy development from their competition governance experience.

## 3. Synthetic Biology Dual-Use

### Key Findings
- Danchin (2025) highlights that combined with pathogen genomic sequences, DNA synthesis makes reconstitution of dangerous organisms possible, raising dual-use concerns.
- The dual-use dilemma: synthetic biology can be used for good (vaccines, biofuels, pharmaceuticals) or misused (bioterrorism, biological weapons).
- NSABB's proposed oversight framework for dual-use research of concern (DURC) relies primarily on local oversight by principal investigators and institutional review.
- European antiterrorism experts have warned that terrorist groups may be able to construct biological weapons in a kitchen as synthetic biology becomes cheaper and more accessible.

## 4. Biosecurity Frameworks for Synthetic Biology

### Key Findings
- NIST is developing comprehensive, scalable, and verifiable synthetic nucleic acid procurement screening mechanisms, partnering with industry stakeholders and implementing monthly testing (August 2025).
- The International Gene Synthesis Consortium (IGSC) adopted Harmonized Screening Protocol v3.0, setting best practices for sequence and customer screening.
- Berman (2026) reviews the convergence of AI and synthetic biology, noting that AI biodesign tools can generate sequences absent from regulatory lists that may nonetheless be dangerous.
- Executive Order 14292 (May 2025) revoked EO 14110 and directed OSTP to revise the Framework for Nucleic Acid Synthesis Screening by August 2025.
- The EU Biotech Act (proposed December 2025) establishes a comprehensive screening framework applicable to any person introducing sequences of concern or benchtop synthesis.

## 5. Synthetic Biology Ethics

### Key Findings
- The Presidential Commission for the Study of Bioethical Issues (2010) recommended "prudent vigilance" and "deliberative democracy" — no new agencies, but coordinated risk assessment, reasonable risk assessment before field release, and ongoing review.
- Douglas (2010) argues the most important ethical issue is the risk that knowledge from synthetic biology will be misused (biological terrorism or warfare).
- The UN Scientific Advisory Board (2025) notes that global regulation via treaty is unlikely short-term, calling for inclusive international ethical guidelines as a basis for good practice.
- Ethical principles include: public beneficence, responsible stewardship, intellectual freedom and responsibility, democratic deliberation, and justice and fairness.

- The Biosecurity Resource Toolbox (EBRF, 2020) provides six categories of tools: risk/threat assessment, biosafety/security training, self-assessment, guidance/best practices, awareness raising, and legislation/policy/codes.

## 7. Biosecurity Hardware Requirements

### Key Findings
- Biological Safety Cabinets (BSCs) are primary containment devices: Class II BSCs provide personnel, environmental, and product protection for low-to-moderate risk agents.
- BSL-4 containment requires Class II BSCs used in suit laboratories with positive-pressure protective suits.
- Facility engineering requirements include: adequate clearance (12-14 inches above cabinet), HEPA filtration, bag-in/bag-out systems for hazardous materials, and building exhaust system integration.
- Hardware includes: HEPA filters, airtight dampers for decontamination, card readers, biometric scanners, and access control systems for personnel/visitor management.

## 8. Biosecurity Cost Analysis

### Key Findings
- Plant biosecurity costs fall into three categories: prevention costs (pathway risk management), surveillance and eradication costs, and costs from established pest impacts.
- Finnish poultry farm study: average preventive biosecurity cost was 3.55 eurocent per bird for broilers (90% CI: 2.56-4.40) and 75.7 eurocent per bird for hatching egg producers.
- Cost components include: preventive medication, pest control, operational hygiene, equipment, insurance, and labor.
- Economies of scale: larger units incur lower biosecurity costs per bird, primarily due to decreasing labor costs.
- Pig farming systematic review (2026): biosecurity implementation incurs upfront infrastructure costs but is often offset by reduced disease losses and increased productivity; adoption remains limited among small-scale producers.

### Citations

## 9. Biosecurity Scalability

### Key Findings
- DNA synthesis technology has evolved from chemical to enzymatic methods, from single-column to massively parallel chip-based approaches, with oligonucleotide lengths increasing from a few hundred bases (2015) to over 1000 (2026).
- Contemporary biosecurity frameworks reflect 20th-century bioweapons programs centered on identifiable pathogens; advances in DNA synthesis, lab automation, and computational design have fundamentally altered list-based approaches.
- AI-biology convergence: AI-enabled capability uplift (ΔAI) is strongest in ideation and design, while experimental validation remains a major bottleneck.
- Relational biosecurity (2026) argues for treating interactions between components as explicit objects of design and governance, addressing how safeguards interact within workflows.
- The AI-biosecurity stack framework: risk and benefit emerge from connections among data, models, agents, laboratory automation, synthesis access, experimental validation, and governance.

## 10. Biosecurity Compliance

### Key Findings
- Australia's Biosecurity Act 2015 provides comprehensive powers: human biosecurity control orders, traveller movement measures, isolation measures, and compliance enforcement through civil penalties and infringement notices.
- The Biosecurity Compliance Audit Program (BCAP) in California requires poultry farms in buffer zones to pass biosecurity audits before receiving indemnity payments for HPAI.
- Audit criteria include: biosecurity plan review, visual verification of perimeter security, line of separation, rodent/wildlife control, documentation/training compliance, and movement tracking.
- Virtual audits are required every 6 months after initial in-person audit until disease-free status is declared.
- The EU Biotech Act (proposed December 2025) establishes comprehensive screening framework for any person introducing sequences of concern or benchtop synthesis.

## Synthesis: Bottlenecks and Challenges

### Key Bottlenecks
1. **Governance gap**: No federal policy in place for review and conduct of dual-use research of concern (DURC) in synthetic biology.
2. **List-based screening limitations**: Current screening relies on known pathogen sequences; AI-designed novel sequences may evade detection.
3. **Decentralized synthesis**: Benchtop DNA synthesis and global market expansion make comprehensive screening difficult.
4. **DIY/amateur biology**: Practitioners outside traditional institutions (students, amateur groups) are not subject to federal biosafety/biosecurity requirements.
5. **International coordination**: No binding global treaty; EU Biotech Act and US frameworks are not harmonized.
6. **Cost barriers**: Small-scale producers face disproportionate biosecurity costs; adoption remains limited.
7. **Scalability of oversight**: Rapidly advancing technology outpaces regulatory framework updates.
8. **AI-biology convergence**: AI biodesign tools lower barriers for designing harmful agents; experimental validation is the current bottleneck.

## Summary

Synthetic biology biosecurity is a rapidly evolving field facing significant governance, technical, and economic challenges. The convergence of AI, DNA synthesis, and lab automation has fundamentally altered the threat landscape, rendering traditional list-based screening approaches insufficient. Key bottlenecks include the lack of federal DURC policy, decentralized synthesis, DIY biology, and international coordination gaps. Cost analyses show economies of scale favor larger operations, while small-scale producers face disproportionate burdens. The field requires coordinated international frameworks, scalable screening mechanisms, and updated oversight paradigms to address emerging dual-use risks.
