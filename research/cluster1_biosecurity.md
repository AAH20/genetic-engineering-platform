# Cluster 1: CRISPR-Cas Systems — Biosecurity & Governance

## Research Synthesis

### 1. CRISPR Biosecurity Review

CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing poses dual-use biosecurity risks by lowering technical barriers to biological weapons development and enabling modification of pathogens, plants, animals, and humans for nefarious purposes [1][2]. The technology's accessibility — CRISPR kits can be ordered commercially and delivery methods are rapidly advancing — means malicious actors need not care about off-target effects as long as disruptive outcomes are achieved [1]. West (2020) outlines how the widespread dissemination of CRISPR techniques has created a landscape where biosecurity concerns are no longer hypothetical but present and near-term [2]. A 2021 review in *Pathogens and Global Health* describes both the promise of CRISPR for global health security (pathogen detection, CRISPR-based antibiotics, gene drives for vector control) and the risks to human health associated with genome-editing tools [3].

### 2. Dual-Use Research of Concern (DURC)

DURC is a narrowly defined US regulatory category requiring a two-part test: (1) research involving one of 15 enumerated select agents/toxins, AND (2) reasonable anticipation of producing knowledge falling into one of seven defined categories of experiments of concern (e.g., enhancing transmissibility/virulence, conferring countermeasure resistance) [4][5]. Most research with listed agents does not meet the second part and is not DURC [5]. The US policy framework evolved through three stages: 2012 USG Policy for Oversight of DURC, 2014 Institutional Oversight policy (requiring Institutional Review Entities by September 2015), and the May 2024 DURC/PEPP policy that replaced and combined earlier frameworks [4][5]. The WHO positions DURC governance as inherently ethical — balancing scientific freedom against security concerns [4]. Precautionary reasoning holds that if significant harm potential exists with no offsetting benefit, DURC research should cease [4].

### 3. Gene Drive Governance

Gene drives intentionally spread genetic traits through populations, distinguishing them from other GMOs and rendering existing governance mechanisms insufficient [6][7]. The US Coordinated Framework splits regulatory authority across FDA, USDA, and EPA, with no single agency holding overarching authority [6]. The 2016 National Academies report *Governing Gene Drive Research and Applications* questioned whether existing governance systems adequately protect public health and environmental interests [6]. iGEM's gene drive governance case study distills six key insights for future policy development [7]. Warmbrod et al. propose a tiered registry system (analogous to ClinicalTrials.gov) for US gene drive governance, noting the US lacks a robust gene drive regulatory system and is not party to the Convention on Biological Diversity [8].

### 4. CRISPR Ethics

Ethical concerns center on: (1) the extent to which CRISPR use should be permitted; (2) access and equitable distribution; (3) regulatory frameworks for human genome editing including germline; and (4) international regulations [9]. The NASEM 2017 report favored somatic genome editing but did not permit enhancement; heritable germline editing might be permissible under strict oversight for compelling reasons [9]. The 2015 Napa Valley meeting initiated international deliberations, leading to the International Summit on Human Gene Editing [9]. Key ethical tensions include: somatic vs. germline editing (heritable changes passed to future generations), enhancement vs. therapy, off-target effects safety, and the risk of consumer-driven eugenics [9][10][11]. The SCIRP paper argues for an international regulatory body as the most balanced path forward [10]. The BenchChem technical guide proposes a structured ethical decision-making framework covering scientific merit, risk-benefit analysis, preclinical safety, informed consent, justice/equity, and stakeholder dialogue [11].

### 5. Biosecurity Frameworks

The FAO defines biosecurity as a strategic, integrated approach to analyze and manage risks in food safety, animal/plant life and health, and biosafety [12]. The FAO Biosecurity Toolkit provides guidance for national frameworks: principles/components, capacity assessment, and risk analysis manuals [12]. The CDC Global Health Security Agenda calls for whole-of-government national biosafety/biosecurity systems ensuring dangerous pathogens are identified, held, secured, and monitored in minimal facilities [13]. The WTO SPS Agreement recognizes CAC, OIE, and IPPC as international standard-setting organizations [14]. Biosecurity plans (distinct from biosafety plans) address: risk assessment, access control, personnel suitability, inventory control, incident response, and information systems security [15]. The Federal Select Agent Program requires written security plans under 42 CFR 73.11, 7 CFR 331.11, and 9 CFR 121.11 [15].

### 6. International Governance of CRISPR

The US operates through a patchwork of overlapping agencies (HHS, CDC, USDA, EPA, DoD) with no single overarching authority [16]. The Biological Weapons Convention (BWC), over 50 years old, lacks verification mechanisms and enforcement powers [16]. The Biosecurity Modernization and Innovation Act of 2026 (Cotton-Klobuchar) represents the most consequential recent legislative attempt to install systematic guardrails [16]. The International Gene Synthesis Consortium's voluntary screening code faces the problem that non-consortium providers have no legal obligation to screen [16]. The WHO 2021 Governance Framework for Human Genome Editing addresses leadership, international collaboration, registries, research oversight, confidential reporting, equitable access, and global dialogue [17][18]. Africa needs a phased, harmonized continental governance framework combining regional oversight, national implementation, and data governance [18]. Turkey's CRISPR governance illustrates the tension between public health and national security logics [17].

### 7. Biosecurity OSS Tools

Sequence-based biosecurity screening software (BSS) used by nucleic acid synthesis providers relies on sequence identity to known sequences of concern (SOCs) [19][20]. Wittmann et al. (2025) demonstrated that AI-assisted protein design (AIPD) tools can reformulate wild-type proteins of concern into synthetic homologs with minimal sequence identity that evade BSS detection [19][20]. A follow-up study found that two of four patched BSS tools could detect fragments as short as 50 nucleotides, but emphasized urgency of developing alternate approaches (e.g., function prediction rather than sequence identity) [20]. Google DeepMind published a protein watermarking system based on SynthID technology that creates undetectable watermarks on protein sequences without compromising function, enabling identification of AI-designed proteins [21]. The BIO-ISAC Biosecurity Evaluation Questionnaire (BSEQ) provides a tool for evaluating OEM security practices in the bioeconomy [22].

### 8. Biosecurity Hardware Requirements

Biosecurity plans require performance-based controls built from site-specific risk assessments [15]. Key hardware elements include: access-control systems (card, biometric, PIN-based readers) with durable exportable audit logs tied to individual credentials; door/alarm integration; inventory-tracking and LIMS platforms with chain-of-custody logging and role-based permissions; and physical security consulting [15]. The BSEQ questionnaire evaluates OEM security practices across organizational security, product security, software development lifecycle, and hosting location [22]. The Biosecurity Handbook addresses biological security in the AI era [23]. Australia's biosecurity system demonstrates the scale of investment: total biosecurity funding of A$792.0M in 2023-24, with cost recovery (A$379.1M) and base appropriation (A$366.4M) as primary sources [24].

### 9. Biosecurity Cost Analysis

Australia's national biosecurity system cost A$792.0M in 2023-24, projected to A$811.3M by 2027-28 [24]. Cost recovery charges for biosecurity regulatory activities increased 3.8% in 2026-27 per legislated indexation [25]. Specific charges include: full import declaration (air: A$48, sea: A$71), permit applications (A$135), horse reservation (A$1,088), and animal confirmation charges up to A$4,447 [25]. The biosecurity services sector includes companies like Biosecurity BV (Italy, founded 2006, 14 employees) providing hygiene scans and risk management tools [26]. The cost of compliance checking is significant — manual compliance review is slow, expensive, and difficult to scale [27]. ARCCS (Automated Regulatory Compliance Checking System) demonstrates that automated compliance can achieve 98.8% accuracy on EU procurement benchmarks and 96.67% on GDPR assessment, suggesting cost reduction potential [27].

### 10. Compliance Checking

Compliance checking is the process of reviewing implemented controls to verify they meet security requirements documented in information security plans and risk treatment plans [28]. Methods include internal/external audits, periodic inspections, management reviews, spot checks, and technical testing [28]. ARCCS presents an end-to-end agentic Legal NLP system that decomposes regulatory text into atomic, traceable requirements and evaluates target documents using retrieved evidence, confidence scores, and human-interpretable justifications [27]. The system achieves 96.67% consistency on GDPR policy evaluation and 98.8% accuracy on EU procurement violation detection [27]. Conformance checking from process mining assesses whether real-world process execution conforms to prescribed normative behavior, applicable both offline (auditing) and online (run-time monitoring) [29]. A key challenge is that the prescriptive model used must itself be proven compliant [29].

## Bottlenecks

1. **Fragmented oversight**: No single agency holds overarching authority for CRISPR governance in the US; authority is split across FDA, USDA, EPA, HHS, and DoD [16]
2. **Voluntary screening**: International Gene Synthesis Consortium screening is voluntary; non-consortium providers face no legal obligation to screen DNA orders [16]
3. **Sequence-based BSS vulnerability**: Current biosecurity screening relies on sequence identity, which AI-assisted protein design can evade through synthetic homologs [19][20]
4. **Governance gap for gene drives**: Existing GMO governance frameworks are insufficient for gene drives; the US lacks a robust gene drive regulatory system [6][8]
5. **No binding international law**: The BWC lacks verification and enforcement mechanisms; no binding international law governs human genome editing [16][18]
6. **Regulation lag**: By the time policymakers began regulating CRISPR, the technology had already diffused globally into hundreds of labs [16]
7. **Compliance checking bottleneck**: Manual compliance review is slow, expensive, and difficult to scale to modern document volumes [27]
8. **Benchtop synthesiser governance**: Unresolved question of how to govern benchtop DNA synthesizers [16]
9. **AI-designed pathogen threat**: AI tools capable of designing novel pathogens that evade current sequence-based detection systems [16]
10. **Off-target effects uncertainty**: Technical limitations (mosaicism, off-target editing) complicate risk/benefit analysis and regulatory decisions [9][11]

## NP-Hard Problems

1. **Protein function prediction**: Predicting whether a synthetic homolog retains hazardous function is computationally intractable for arbitrary protein sequences [19][20]
2. **Regulatory compliance verification**: Determining whether a document satisfies all obligations of a regulation with cross-referential legal text is NP-hard [27]
3. **Gene drive population dynamics**: Modeling the spread of gene drives through populations with ecological feedback is computationally complex [6]
4. **Off-target effect prediction**: Genome-wide prediction of CRISPR off-target sites with high accuracy is NP-hard [9][11]
5. **Sequence screening optimization**: Optimal screening of all possible synthetic homologs against all known SOCs is combinatorially explosive [19][20]

## Failure Modes

1. **Synthetic homolog evasion**: AI-designed proteins with minimal sequence identity evade BSS detection [19][20]
2. **Non-consortium synthesis**: Dangerous sequences obtained from providers outside voluntary screening consortiums [16]
3. **Regulatory arbitrage**: Research moved to jurisdictions with weaker oversight [16][18]
4. **Germline editing misuse**: Heritable genome editing performed outside regulatory oversight (e.g., He Jiankui case) [17]
5. **Gene drive unintended spread**: Gene drives escaping intended populations or ecosystems [6][8]
6. **Off-target mutations**: Unintended CRISPR edits causing oncogene activation or essential gene disruption [9][11]
7. **Compliance model drift**: Prescriptive compliance models themselves becoming non-compliant [29]
8. **Fragmented oversight gaps**: Research falling through cracks between agencies [16]
9. **AI-designed pathogens**: Novel pathogens designed by AI evading sequence-based detection [16]
10. **Inventory control failures**: Theft or loss of select agents due to inadequate chain-of-custody [15]
